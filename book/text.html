The sxml Book

Working with Text

Text is where software meets people, and people write in many languages. This chapter covers three things sxml does with text beyond plain strings: sorting and comparing it the way a language expects, searching it by words instead of characters, and writing dates and numbers in other languages. The examples run on the Vane draft, in docs/book/examples/spec.

Collations

By default, strings sort by their Unicode code points. That puts every capital letter before every small letter, so the terms of the draft and the words that refer to them sort into two runs:

$ sxml xpath --context-item=vane.xml 'sort(distinct-values((//dt, //xref[starts-with(@target, "term-")]) ! string()))'
"Flag"
"Pause"
"Percentage"
"SDK"
"flag"
"pause"
"percentage"

A collation is a set of rules for comparing strings. Every function and instruction that compares strings (sort, compare, distinct-values, order by, xsl:sort, index-of and more) can take one, named by a URI. The Unicode Collation Algorithm (UCA) gives the order people expect:

$ sxml xpath --context-item=vane.xml 'sort(distinct-values((//dt, //xref[starts-with(@target, "term-")]) ! string()), "http://www.w3.org/2013/collation/UCA")'
"flag"
"Flag"
"pause"
"Pause"
"percentage"
"Percentage"
"SDK"

Parameters in the URI tailor the order. strength says which differences count: primary ignores accents and case, secondary ignores only case. With it, distinct-values finds that the draft has four terms, however a sentence capitalizes them:

$ sxml xpath --context-item=vane.xml 'distinct-values((//dt, //xref[starts-with(@target, "term-")]) ! string(), "http://www.w3.org/2013/collation/UCA?strength=secondary")'
"Flag"
"SDK"
"Percentage"
"Pause"
$ sxml xpath 'compare("Strasse", "Straße", "http://www.w3.org/2013/collation/UCA?strength=primary")'
0

numeric=yes sorts digits by their numeric value. The anchors of the requirements end in numbers, and by code points req-10 comes before req-2:

$ sxml xpath --context-item=vane.xml 'string-join(sort(//t/@anchor ! string()), " ")'
"req-1 req-10 req-11 req-2 req-3 req-4 req-5 req-6 req-7 req-8 req-9"
$ sxml xpath --context-item=vane.xml 'string-join(sort(//t/@anchor ! string(), "http://www.w3.org/2013/collation/UCA?numeric=yes"), " ")'
"req-1 req-2 req-3 req-4 req-5 req-6 req-7 req-8 req-9 req-10 req-11"

Punctuation counts, too. The authors of the references include M'Raihi, and by default the apostrophe sorts before every letter, so M'Raihi comes before Machani. alternate=shifted makes punctuation and spaces count only when everything else is equal, as a reference list or a phone book expects:

$ sxml xpath --context-item=vane.xml 'string-join(sort(distinct-values(//reference//author/@surname), "http://www.w3.org/2013/collation/UCA"), ", ")'
"Bradner, Crocker, Leiba, M'Raihi, Machani, Overell, Pei, Rydell"
$ sxml xpath --context-item=vane.xml 'string-join(sort(distinct-values(//reference//author/@surname), "http://www.w3.org/2013/collation/UCA?alternate=shifted"), ", ")'
"Bradner, Crocker, Leiba, Machani, M'Raihi, Overell, Pei, Rydell"

lang uses the rules of a language. The draft is in English, but a translation of its terms would not be. In Spanish, ñ is a letter of its own, after n:

$ sxml xpath 'sort(("llamada", "luz", "bandera", "función", "ñu", "nube"), "http://www.w3.org/2013/collation/UCA?lang=es")'
"bandera"
"función"
"llamada"
"luz"
"nube"
"ñu"

German has two orders. In the phone book order, ü sorts as ue:

$ sxml xpath 'sort(("Müller", "Mueller", "Muller"), "http://www.w3.org/2013/collation/UCA?lang=de")'
"Mueller"
"Muller"
"Müller"
$ sxml xpath 'sort(("Müller", "Mueller", "Muller"), "http://www.w3.org/2013/collation/UCA?lang=de-u-co-phonebk")'
"Mueller"
"Müller"
"Muller"

sxml carries the root collation of the Unicode CLDR, and the tailorings for English, German, French, Italian, Spanish and Dutch with their regional variants. Other languages use the root order.

Full-text search

contains finds characters. Searching for words is a different problem: "pause" should find "paused", a phrase should match its words in order, and two words should be near each other. XQuery and XPath Full Text adds the contains text expression for this:

$ sxml xpath --context-item=vane.xml 'string-join(//t[@anchor][. contains text "pause"]/@anchor, " ")'
"req-5 req-7 req-8 req-11"

The search is by words and ignores case, so it found "pause" in "in a pause" and in "A pause SHOULD last", but not "paused", the state that req-1 names: that is another word. Options after using change that. With stemming, words match by their stem:

$ sxml xpath --context-item=vane.xml 'string-join(//t[@anchor][. contains text "pause" using stemming]/@anchor, " ")'
"req-1 req-5 req-7 req-8 req-11"

Now req-1 matches too. A phrase in quotes matches its words in order, and ftand, ftor and ftnot combine searches:

$ sxml xpath --context-item=vane.xml '//t[@anchor][. contains text "status request"]/@anchor/string()'
"req-6"
"req-10"
$ sxml xpath --context-item=vane.xml 'string-join(//t[@anchor][. contains text "flag" ftand "pause"]/@anchor, " ")'
"req-5 req-7 req-11"
$ sxml xpath --context-item=vane.xml 'string-join(//t[@anchor][. contains text "pause" ftand ftnot "flag"]/@anchor, " ")'
"req-8"

distance and window say how close words must be, and wildcards makes a .* in a word match any characters, so roll.* finds "rollout" and "rolling-out" as well as "roll":

$ sxml xpath --context-item=vane.xml 'string-join(//t[@anchor][. contains text "flag" ftand "pause" distance at most 3 words]/@anchor, " ")'
"req-7"
$ sxml xpath --context-item=vane.xml 'string-join(//t[@anchor][. contains text "roll.*" using wildcards]/@anchor, " ")'
"req-1 req-3 req-4 req-5 req-9 req-6"

Only req-7, "put the flag in a pause", has the two words close together; req-5 and req-11 have them further apart. The wildcard finds "rolling-out" in req-1, "rollout" in req-4, req-6 and req-9, both in req-3, and "roll out" in req-5.

A score variable in a for clause gives each result a relevance between 0 and 1, to rank the results:

$ sxml xquery --context-item=vane.xml --method=adaptive --expression='
    for $t score $score in //t[@anchor][. contains text "rollout" ftor "status"]
    order by $score descending
    return $t/@anchor || " " || round($score, 2)'
"req-6 0.26"
"req-9 0.16"
"req-3 0.16"
"req-4 0.16"
"req-10 0.15"

req-6 has both words, "status request" and "rollout step", and comes first. The others have one of the words once each, and then the length of the paragraph decides: req-10 has "status" in 22 words, more than any of the other three, and comes last. using language "de" selects the language of the words, for stemming and stop words. sxml has stemmers for English, German, French, Italian, Spanish and Dutch, and reads lists of stop words and thesauri from files. contains text works in XQuery as in XPath, as the last example shows.

Dates in other languages

format-date, format-time and format-dateTime write dates with a picture string. Each part in square brackets is a component: [D] the day, [MNn] the month's name, [FNn] the day of the week, [Y] the year:

$ sxml xpath "format-date(xs:date('2026-10-21'), '[FNn], [D] [MNn] [Y]')"
"Wednesday, 21 October 2026"
$ sxml xpath "format-date(xs:date('2026-10-21'), '[D1o] [MNn], [Y]')"
"21st October, 2026"
$ sxml xpath "format-date(xs:date('2026-10-21'), '[Y0001]-[M01]-[D01], week [W]')"
"2026-10-21, week 43"

The draft writes its date as three attributes, with the month as an English name. A query makes an xs:date of it, and then writes it in three languages: the third argument of format-date is a language, and n instead of Nn writes a name in small letters, as French does:

text-date.xq:

(: The date of the draft, written in English, German and French. The
   date element has the year, the month's name and the day. :)
let $date := /rfc/front/date
let $months := ("January", "February", "March", "April", "May", "June", "July",
                "August", "September", "October", "November", "December")
let $month := index-of($months, $date/@month)
let $value := xs:date(`{$date/@year}-{format-integer($month, "01")}-{format-integer($date/@day, "01")}`)
return (
  format-date($value, "[D] [MNn] [Y]", "en", (), ()),
  format-date($value, "[FNn], [D]. [MNn] [Y]", "de", (), ()),
  format-date($value, "[Fn] [D] [Mn] [Y]", "fr", (), ())
)

$ sxml xquery --method=adaptive --context-item=vane.xml text-date.xq
"21 October 2026"
"Mittwoch, 21. Oktober 2026"
"mercredi 21 octobre 2026"
$ sxml xquery --method=adaptive --context-item=vane-00.xml text-date.xq
"6 July 2026"
"Montag, 6. Juli 2026"
"lundi 6 juillet 2026"

The language can have a region, and the fourth argument is a calendar. In Austria, January is "Jänner", as in the month of RFC 5234; JE is the calendar of Japanese eras:

$ sxml xpath "format-date(xs:date('2008-01-01'), '[MNn] [Y]', 'de-AT', (), ())"
"Jänner 2008"
$ sxml xpath "format-date(xs:date('2026-10-21'), '[E] [Y] [MNn] [D]', 'en', 'JE', ())"
"Reiwa 8 October 21"

The fifth argument is a place: a time zone name from the IANA Time Zone Database, which sxml reads from /usr/share/zoneinfo. A pause that begins at 14:30 UTC begins at 10:30 for an owner of the flag in New York:

$ sxml xpath "format-dateTime(xs:dateTime('2026-10-21T14:30:00Z'), '[H01]:[m01] [ZN]', 'en', (), 'America/New_York')"
"10:30 EDT"
$ sxml xpath "civil-timezone(xs:dateTime('2026-07-06T12:00:00'), 'Europe/Lisbon')"
xs:dayTimeDuration("PT1H")

civil-timezone gives the offset of a place at a moment, summer time included.

Numbers in words

format-integer writes numbers as words, by the rules of the Unicode CLDR for each language, and ordinals with o:

$ sxml xpath "format-integer(11, 'w'), format-integer(11, 'w', 'de'), format-integer(11, 'Ww;o', 'fr')"
"eleven"
"elf"
"Onzième"

The draft writes its numbers in words: ten minutes, three failed checks, five minutes. The Vane service, which implements it, has them as numbers in its rollout module. A query can check that each requirement says the number the service uses, with format-integer making the word and contains text finding it (an expression in braces is searched for):

text-numbers.xq:

(: The flag's numbers, and the requirement that states each of them in
   words: ten minutes, three failed checks, five minutes. :)
let $numbers := { "req-3": 10, "req-7": 3, "req-8": 5 }
for $anchor in map:keys($numbers)
let $words := format-integer($numbers($anchor), "w")
let $requirement := //t[@anchor = $anchor]
return `{$anchor} states {$words}: {$requirement contains text { $words }}`

$ sxml xquery --method=adaptive --context-item=vane.xml text-numbers.xq
"req-3 states ten: true"
"req-7 states three: true"
"req-8 states five: true"
$ sxml xquery --method=adaptive --context-item=vane-00.xml text-numbers.xq
"req-3 states ten: true"
"req-7 states three: false"
"req-8 states five: false"

The first draft said "five" failed checks and "one minute"; the second agrees with the service.

format-number writes decimals with grouping and a fixed number of places. Its options choose the separators:

$ sxml xpath "format-number(5 div 11, '0.0%')"
"45.5%"
$ sxml xpath "format-number(300000, '#,##0'), format-number(300000, '#.##0', { 'decimal-separator': ',', 'grouping-separator': '.' })"
"300,000"
"300.000"

Five of the eleven requirements are MUSTs, and a pause of five minutes is 300,000 milliseconds, or 300.000 in German. In XSLT, xsl:number and xsl:sort take a lang attribute and use the same data.

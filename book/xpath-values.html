The sxml Book

Values and Types

Every atomic value in XPath has a type. You rarely have to write a type down, but knowing they exist explains most of the surprises you'll meet.

Literals

Numbers and strings can be written directly. The way a number is written decides its type:

$ sxml xpath '42, 3.14, 1e3, "MUST", true()'
42
3.14
1.0e3
"MUST"
true()

42 is an xs:integer, 3.14 an xs:decimal and 1e3 an xs:double. Integers and decimals are exact: sxml computes with them digit by digit, however large they get. Doubles are the floating-point numbers of most programming languages, with their usual rounding:

$ sxml xpath '0.1 + 0.2, 0.1e0 + 0.2e0'
0.3
3.0000000000000004e-1
$ sxml xpath '9999999999999999999 + 1'
10000000000000000000

sxml writes doubles in exponent notation, so you can always tell them apart. true() and false() are the two booleans; they are functions because true alone would be the name of an element.

type-of tells you the type of any value:

$ sxml xpath 'type-of(1), type-of(1.0), type-of("1"), type-of(1e0)'
"xs:integer"
"xs:decimal"
"xs:string"
"xs:double"

Arithmetic

+, - and * are what you expect. Division is div, because / already separates steps. idiv divides whole numbers and mod gives the remainder:

$ sxml xpath '7 div 2, 7 idiv 2, 7 mod 2'
3.5
3
1

count returns an integer, so arithmetic on counts is exact. What share of the draft's eleven requirements are MUSTs?

$ sxml xpath --context-item=vane.xml 'count(//bcp14[. = "MUST"]), count(//bcp14[. = "MUST"]) div count(//t[@anchor]), count(//bcp14[. = "MUST"]) * 100 idiv count(//t[@anchor])'
5
0.4545454545454545454545454545454545
45

Five of eleven: dividing two integers with div gives a decimal, with as many digits as sxml keeps, and idiv gives the whole percentage. Functions and Operators shows how to write such a number nicely.

Dividing an integer or a decimal by zero is an error. Dividing a double by zero gives infinity, as floating-point numbers do:

$ sxml xpath '1 div 0'
expression:1:1: err:FOAR0001: division by zero (F&O 4.0, op:numeric-divide, op:numeric-integer-divide, op:numeric-mod)
$ sxml xpath '1e0 div 0'
INF

Untyped values

A plain XML document has no types in it. The year attribute of <date year="1997" month="March"/> is just the characters 1, 9, 9, 7. XPath gives such values the type xs:untypedAtomic; data takes the value out of a node:

$ sxml xpath --context-item=vane.xml 'type-of((//reference)[1]//date/@year), type-of(data((//reference)[1]//date/@year))'
"attribute()"
"xs:untypedAtomic"

An untyped value is converted when it is used:

  • in arithmetic, it becomes an xs:double;
  • compared with a number using = or <, it becomes a number too;
  • compared with a string, it is compared as a string.

That is why the age of each reference, at the draft's date in 2026, comes out as doubles:

$ sxml xpath --context-item=vane.xml '//reference ! (2026 - .//date/@year)'
2.9e1
1.8e1
9.0e0
1.5e1

RFC 2119 is 29 years old. When you want exact arithmetic, convert the values first with a constructor function, named after the type:

$ sxml xpath --context-item=vane.xml '//reference ! (2026 - xs:integer(.//date/@year))'
29
18
9
15

Constructor functions exist for every type: xs:string, xs:integer, xs:decimal, xs:double, xs:boolean, xs:date, xs:dateTime, xs:duration, xs:gYear and many more. When the text is not a valid value of the type, they raise an error:

$ sxml xpath 'xs:integer("three")'
expression:1:1: err:FORG0001: the value is not in the lexical space or value space of the target type

castable as asks first, and cast as is another way to write the conversion:

$ sxml xpath '"three" castable as xs:integer, "3" cast as xs:integer'
false()
3

Validating with XSD shows how a schema gives the values of a document their types, so that a year is a year without any conversion.

Two kinds of comparison

XPath has two sets of comparison operators, and the difference matters.

The general comparisons, =, !=, <, <=, > and >=, compare sequences. They are true if any pair of items compares true:

$ sxml xpath --context-item=vane.xml '//bcp14 = "SHALL"'
false()
$ sxml xpath --context-item=vane.xml '//bcp14 = ("MUST", "SHALL")'
true()

The first asks "does any key word say SHALL?". BCP 14 allows SHALL as another way to write MUST, and this draft never uses it. The second asks whether any key word is MUST or SHALL. This is what makes predicates over documents so convenient: a reference may have several dates or authors, and a test such as [.//date/@year < 2000] is true if one of them matches. Here the untyped year is compared with a number, as a number:

$ sxml xpath --context-item=vane.xml '//reference[.//date/@year < 2000]/@anchor/string()'
"RFC2119"
$ sxml xpath --context-item=vane.xml '//reference[.//date/@year = (1997, 2017)]/@anchor/string()'
"RFC2119"
"RFC8174"

The two references of BCP 14 are from 1997 and 2017. It also means != is not the opposite of =: //bcp14 != "MUST" is true, because there are key words that are not MUST.

$ sxml xpath --context-item=vane.xml '//bcp14 != "MUST"'
true()

The value comparisons, eq, ne, lt, le, gt and ge, compare exactly one item with one item, and they are strict about types. An untyped value is compared as a string, so comparing an attribute with a number is an error:

$ sxml xpath --context-item=vane.xml '/rfc/front/date/@year eq 2026'
expression:1:1: err:XPTY0004: the compared items belong to different type families (XPath 4.0, section 4.11.1; F&O 4.0, fn:compare)
$ sxml xpath --context-item=vane.xml 'xs:integer(/rfc/front/date/@year) eq 2026'
true()

Use general comparisons in predicates over documents, and value comparisons when you know you have single values of known types.

Strings compare by their characters. Numbers compare as numbers. That makes a difference, as anyone who has sorted RFC numbers as text knows:

$ sxml xpath '"RFC10" lt "RFC9", 10 lt 9'
true()
false()

Dates, times and durations

Dates, times and durations are types of their own, with arithmetic that knows about calendars. The draft writes its date the xml2rfc way, with the month as a word:

    <date year="2026" month="October" day="21"/>

To make an xs:date from it, we need the month's number. A map is a lookup table, written with curly braces, and calling it with a key gives the value; Maps, Arrays and Records explains maps fully. format-integer writes the numbers with two digits, and the string between backticks is a string template, which puts the values of the expressions in curly braces into a string:

$ sxml xpath --context-item=vane.xml 'let $months := { "January": 1, "February": 2, "March": 3, "April": 4, "May": 5, "June": 6, "July": 7, "August": 8, "September": 9, "October": 10, "November": 11, "December": 12 } let $date := /rfc/front/date return xs:date(`{$date/@year}-{format-integer($months($date/@month), "00")}-{format-integer($date/@day, "00")}`)'
xs:date("2026-10-21")

sxml writes dates as constructor calls, so you can paste a result back into an expression. let binds a name to a value; it comes back in Functions and Operators.

The previous version of the draft is in vane-00.xml. doc reads another document, so one expression can look at both. Here are both dates, -00 first, and the time between them (this time the map has only the two months it needs):

$ sxml xpath --context-item=vane.xml 'let $months := { "July": 7, "October": 10 } let $dates := (doc("vane-00.xml"), /) ! rfc/front/date ! xs:date(`{@year}-{format-integer($months(@month), "00")}-{format-integer(@day, "00")}`) return ($dates, $dates[2] - $dates[1])'
xs:date("2026-07-06")
xs:date("2026-10-21")
xs:dayTimeDuration("P107D")

Subtracting two dates gives a duration. Durations are written as in ISO 8601: P107D is 107 days. Adding a duration to a date gives a date. An Internet-Draft expires 185 days after it is posted, and a month later is a month later, whatever its length:

$ sxml xpath 'xs:date("2026-10-21") + xs:dayTimeDuration("P185D")'
xs:date("2027-04-24")
$ sxml xpath 'xs:date("2026-01-31") + xs:yearMonthDuration("P1M")'
xs:date("2026-02-28")
$ sxml xpath 'xs:dateTime("2026-10-21T09:30:00Z") + xs:dayTimeDuration("PT90M")'
xs:dateTime("2026-10-21T11:00:00Z")

PT90M is 90 minutes: the T separates the date part of a duration from its time part.

Effective boolean values

A predicate or an if needs true or false. When the value is something else, XPath takes its effective boolean value: the empty sequence is false, a sequence that starts with a node is true, a string is true unless it is empty, a number is true unless it is zero or NaN.

$ sxml xpath --context-item=vane.xml 'boolean(//t[@anchor = "req-12"]), boolean(//cref), boolean(""), boolean("false"), boolean(0)'
false()
true()
false()
true()
false()

There is no req-12, so the first is false; the draft has editorial notes, so the second is true. Note the fourth one: the string "false" is not empty, so it is true. This is what lets a predicate such as [@anchor] mean "has an anchor": the path finds the attribute or nothing.

Sequence types

Sometimes you write a type down: in instance of, in a function's signature, or in a variable declaration in XQuery. A sequence type is an item type with an optional occurrence indicator: ? for zero or one, * for any number, + for one or more.

$ sxml xpath '"MUST" instance of xs:string+, () instance of xs:string?'
true()
true()
$ sxml xpath --context-item=vane.xml '//t[bcp14][not(@anchor)] instance of element(t)?, //bcp14 instance of element(bcp14)+'
true()
true()

The draft has at most one paragraph with a key word and no anchor (it has exactly one), and one or more bcp14 elements. element(bcp14) is the type of elements named bcp14; node(), attribute(), text() and the other kind tests are types too. You'll use sequence types throughout XQuery and XSLT.
